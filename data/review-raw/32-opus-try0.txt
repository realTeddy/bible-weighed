  "bottom_line": "The book of Daniel's 'predictions' are vague about the Babylonian and Persian periods. They grow sharper and more accurate about the Hellenistic period, and they reach close detail about events under the Seleucid king Antiochus IV Epiphanes, including the desecration of the Jerusalem temple in 167 BC. They then go wrong about how Antiochus would die (Daniel 11:40-45), and they show no knowledge of the temple's rededication in December 164 BC. That pattern is the standard signature of a text written during the crisis and set back in an earlier era, which was a common convention in Jewish apocalyptic writing. It points strongly to composition around 167-164 BC. Several other lines of evidence point the same way: problems with the 6th-century setting (Darius the Mede, the dating in Daniel 1:1), Greek and Persian loanwords, the book's placement among the Writings rather than the Prophets, and its absence from Ben Sira's list of Israel's heroes around 180 BC. The court stories in chapters 1-6 probably circulated earlier, likely in the Persian or early Hellenistic period, but not as 6th-century writings by Daniel. This is the consensus of critical scholarship, including most Catholic and many evangelical scholars. A minority of conservative scholars defend the traditional date, largely by reading chapters 11:36-45 as about a far-future Antichrist."
